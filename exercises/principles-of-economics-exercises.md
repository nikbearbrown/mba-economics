## Chapter 01: Chapter 1 — Welcome to Economics
*Source: `chapters/01-welcome-to-economics.md`*

## Exercises

**Warm-up**

*1. Scarcity in your own day.* List three choices you made today — what you ate, how you spent an hour, what you did not do. For each, identify the resource that was scarce and name the opportunity cost. The goal is not to be clever. The goal is to see that the framework fits ordinary life without straining it. *(Tests: scarcity as a universal condition; opportunity cost as a different kind of cost.)*

*2. Poverty, shortage, or scarcity?* Classify each of the following as primarily a problem of poverty, shortage, or scarcity — and explain in one sentence why the other two labels do not fit: (a) A family cannot afford insulin. (b) A city ran out of hotel rooms during the Olympics. (c) A billionaire cannot attend two parties on the same night. *(Tests: precision of the three definitions.)*

*3. Read the circular flow.* Draw the circular flow from memory. Then trace a single dollar bill starting from a household's paycheck: which arrows does it travel, in what direction, and where does it end up? *(Tests: mechanical understanding of the circular flow model.)*

**Application**

*4. The opportunity cost you cannot see.* A university owns its campus buildings outright and pays no rent. A student group proposes a new program that would use one building full-time. The administration says, "It won't cost anything — the building is already ours." Is the administration right? Explain what cost they are omitting, and how an economist would calculate it. *(Tests: implicit opportunity cost; applying the concept to a situation where no money changes hands.)*

*5. Comparative advantage with numbers.* A translator can proofread 10 pages per hour or translate 4 pages per hour. A junior editor can proofread 6 pages per hour or translate 1 page per hour. Who has the comparative advantage in translation? Who should do the proofreading? Show your reasoning step by step — do not just state the answer. *(Tests: applying comparative advantage beyond the surgeon/carpenter example; numerical reasoning.)*

*6. Pick the right lens.* You are asked three questions: (a) Why did egg prices spike in early 2023? (b) Why was U.S. unemployment lower in 2019 than in 2010? (c) Will a new local minimum wage reduce teen employment in this city? For each, say whether you would reach for a micro or macro lens first, and name the feature of the question that makes one lens more appropriate. *(Tests: distinguishing micro from macro by question type, not topic name.)*

**Synthesis**

*7. The pin factory in the 21st century.* The 2021 global chip shortage was described as structurally similar to the pin factory. Explain that structural similarity precisely: which of the three mechanisms of specialization were at work in the semiconductor industry, and how did each contribute to the shortage's severity? Then identify which mechanism most explains why the shortage persisted for over a year rather than resolving quickly. *(Tests: applying all three mechanisms to a real case; distinguishing their individual contributions.)*

*8. The coordination problem in practice.* In spring 2020, U.S. grocery stores ran out of flour while commercial bakeries had surplus they could not move, because the two supply chains ran on completely separate distribution networks. Analyze this event: what type of coordination was the grocery supply chain using, why did it fail at this specific task, and what type of coordination would have resolved the mismatch faster? *(Tests: applying the three coordination systems to a real event; identifying when market coordination fails and why.)*

**Challenge**

*9. Where the circular flow breaks.* The simple circular flow has no government, no banks, and no foreign trade. Choose one of these omissions and describe a real economic event from the past twenty years that the simple model cannot explain — not just "fails to include" but actively predicts wrong. Then sketch, in words, what you would need to add to the model to account for it. What does that addition cost you in terms of the model's clarity and usability? *(Tests: critical evaluation of a model on its own terms; trade-off thinking about model design.)*

*10. The honest verdict.* The chapter ends with two admissions: that comparative advantage is well-established, and that why decentralized markets work as well as they do is not fully understood. Find one other claim in this chapter that you think deserves a similar epistemic flag — something asserted more confidently than the evidence warrants. Make the argument for why it should carry more uncertainty. Then consider: does adding that uncertainty change any practical conclusion a student should draw? *(Tests: intellectual honesty applied to course material; distinguishing empirical claims from theoretical ones.)*

## Chapter 03: Chapter 3 — Demand and Supply
*Source: `chapters/03-demand-and-supply.md`*

## Exercises

**Warm-up**

*1. Draw and label.* From memory, draw a demand-and-supply diagram. Label: both curves, both axes, the equilibrium price and quantity. Then add a second equilibrium point showing what happens when demand shifts right. Name the shift variable that caused it and state whether price and quantity rose or fell. *(Tests: mechanical ability to construct and read the diagram.)*

*2. Classify the change.* For each of the following, identify whether it causes a movement along the demand curve, a shift of the demand curve, or a shift of the supply curve — and name the specific shift variable if it's a shift: (a) the price of coffee rises and people buy less coffee; (b) a study links coffee to improved memory and demand rises; (c) a drought destroys the Colombian coffee harvest; (d) the price of tea falls (a substitute for coffee). *(Tests: the movement-along vs. shift-of distinction, which is the most common source of student error.)*

*3. Law of supply.* Explain in your own words why the supply curve slopes upward. Then name one real-world industry where you think the slope is very steep (supply responds little to price) and one where it is very flat (supply responds a lot). What determines the difference? *(Tests: understanding the mechanism behind the law of supply, not just the label.)*

**Application**

*4. Run the four-step.* In 2020, COVID-19 shut down most restaurants. Apply the four-step analysis to the market for commercial restaurant equipment. Identify the shift variable, which curve moved and in which direction, and predict what happened to equilibrium price and quantity. Then check: did used restaurant equipment prices rise or fall in 2020? Does the model's prediction match? *(Tests: applying the four-step to a real event; connecting model prediction to observable outcome.)*

*5. Complements and substitutes.* Electric vehicles and home charging stations are complements. Gasoline-powered cars and EVs are substitutes. If the federal government ends the $7,500 EV tax credit, trace the effect through both relationships: what happens to the market for home charging stations, and what happens to the market for gasoline-powered cars? Run a separate four-step for each market. *(Tests: using substitute/complement relationships across multiple markets; applying the four-step twice in sequence.)*

*6. Price floor consequences.* A city sets a minimum price for rideshare rides of $12, above the current equilibrium of $8. Using the diagram, predict: (a) whether a shortage or surplus results and why; (b) who benefits and who is harmed; (c) one unintended consequence the city council probably did not announce when passing the policy. *(Tests: price floor mechanics; connecting diagram outcomes to real distributional consequences.)*

**Synthesis**

*7. Two shifts, one ambiguous outcome.* In early 2022, the war in Ukraine simultaneously disrupted global wheat exports (supply shock) and prompted some countries to increase wheat stockpiling (demand shock). For global wheat prices: state which direction price moved and explain why that prediction is unambiguous. For global wheat quantity traded: explain why the model cannot tell you the direction without additional information, and name what information you would need to resolve the ambiguity. *(Tests: handling simultaneous shifts; distinguishing pinned predictions from ambiguous ones.)*

*8. Read the surplus.* A pharmaceutical company holds a patent on a drug. Patients who need it would pay up to $500/dose; the company charges $200. The marginal cost of production is $15/dose. On a diagram, sketch the consumer surplus, producer surplus, and identify whether total surplus is maximized. Now suppose a price ceiling of $50/dose is imposed. Trace what happens to each surplus triangle and identify the deadweight loss. Whose surplus shifted, whose evaporated, and what transactions no longer happen? *(Tests: surplus analysis applied to a real policy context; connecting the geometry to the distributional story.)*

**Challenge**

*9. When the model misfires.* The chapter notes that the minimum wage empirical literature is messier than the simple price-floor diagram predicts. Identify one specific assumption built into the standard supply-and-demand model that, if relaxed, could explain why modest minimum wage increases sometimes produce little measurable unemployment. Explain the mechanism — don't just name the assumption. Then consider: does this make the supply-and-demand model wrong, or does it make it incomplete? What's the difference? *(Tests: critical evaluation of model assumptions; distinguishing "wrong" from "incomplete" as categories of model failure.)*

*10. The organic apple, fully explained.* The chapter opens with the organic vs. conventional apple price puzzle and closes by resolving it. Now extend the analysis: suppose a new study convincingly links conventional pesticides to health risks, and consumer concern shifts demand for organic apples sharply right at the same moment a new organic certification process cuts certification costs by 40%, shifting organic supply right as well. For organic apple prices: is the new equilibrium price higher, lower, or ambiguous? For organic apple quantity: is it higher, lower, or ambiguous? Show your reasoning with the two-shift framework, and name what additional information would resolve any ambiguity. *(Tests: fluent use of the two-shift framework on a novel case derived from the chapter's hook.)*

## Chapter 05: Chapter 5 — Elasticity
*Source: `chapters/05-elasticity.md`*

## Exercises

**Warm-up**

*1. Calculate and classify.* The price of a concert ticket rises from $80 to $100 (a 25 percent increase). Ticket sales fall from 4,000 to 3,400 (a 15 percent decrease). Calculate the price elasticity of demand using the percentage formula. Is demand elastic, inelastic, or unitary? Based only on this number, did the venue's total revenue rise or fall? Show your arithmetic. *(Tests: computing elasticity; applying the revenue rule.)*

*2. Slope and shape.* Draw two demand curves on the same axes passing through the same point — one steep, one shallow. Label which is more elastic and which is more inelastic. Then answer: if a $5 price increase is applied to both, which curve loses more quantity? Use the diagram to explain why, in one sentence, without using the word "slope." *(Tests: visual interpretation of elasticity; distinction between steep/shallow and elastic/inelastic.)*

*3. Classify three goods.* For each of the following, predict whether demand is more elastic or more inelastic, and name the dominant reason (substitutes, budget share, or time horizon): (a) emergency-room visits; (b) a specific brand of luxury handbag; (c) gasoline in the week after a price spike; (d) gasoline five years after a price spike. *(Tests: applying the three determinants; noting how time horizon changes the classification for the same good.)*

**Application**

*4. The revenue gamble.* A regional airline is considering raising ticket prices 20 percent on its most popular route. An analyst estimates demand elasticity on that route is 0.6. A second analyst says the route's elasticity is 1.4. (a) For each estimate, predict whether revenue rises or falls and by approximately how much. (b) Which analyst's estimate makes the price increase a good idea? (c) What information about the route would help you decide which analyst is right? *(Tests: applying the revenue rule with numerical elasticities; reasoning about what determines elasticity in a specific market.)*

*5. Who bears the gas tax?* The federal gasoline tax in the U.S. is levied on refiners (sellers). Short-run demand elasticity for gasoline is approximately 0.2; short-run supply elasticity is approximately 0.4. Use the incidence rule to estimate what fraction of the tax is borne by consumers versus producers in the short run. Then repeat the analysis assuming long-run elasticities of 0.7 (demand) and 1.2 (supply). How does the incidence shift? *(Tests: applying the incidence rule numerically; connecting short-run vs. long-run elasticity to policy consequences.)*

*6. Netflix 2022.* By 2022, Netflix faced meaningful competition from Disney+, HBO Max, Apple TV+, and Peacock. In January 2022, Netflix raised prices and lost 200,000 subscribers in Q1 — its first subscriber loss in a decade. Apply the elasticity framework: (a) What changed about Netflix's market structure between 2011 and 2022 that changed the relevant elasticity? (b) Was Netflix's demand elastic or inelastic in 2022 by the time losses appeared? (c) What does this imply about what Netflix's revenue did in Q1 2022? Check whether your prediction matches the reported outcome. *(Tests: elasticity as a market property not a product property; connecting substitutes and time to observable outcomes.)*

**Synthesis**

*7. The sugar tax paradox.* Several U.S. cities have levied taxes on sweetened beverages, legally imposed on distributors, with two stated goals: (a) raise revenue, and (b) reduce consumption of sugary drinks. Using the elasticity framework, explain under what conditions these two goals are in tension with each other. Specifically: what elasticity makes the tax good at raising revenue but bad at reducing consumption? What does the empirical evidence on soda demand elasticity suggest about which goal is actually being achieved? *(Tests: applying elasticity to policy goals that point in opposite directions; distinguishing revenue and behavior-change objectives.)*

*8. Payroll tax incidence.* The U.S. Social Security payroll tax is legally split 50-50 between workers and employers. Economists estimate that labor supply elasticity is approximately 0.1–0.2 and labor demand elasticity is approximately 0.3–0.5. (a) Using these ranges, estimate the fraction of the tax borne by workers. (b) Is the actual incidence closer to the legal split (50-50) or more tilted toward workers? (c) What would have to be true about labor markets for the legal split to actually equal the economic incidence? *(Tests: numerical incidence calculation; connecting labor market structure to policy claims about who a tax "hits.")*

**Challenge**

*9. When elasticity changes the moral argument.* The chapter notes that goods with inelastic demand are often necessities — gasoline, insulin, water — and that taxing them is effective for revenue but regressive. A policymaker argues: "We should tax luxury goods instead — their demand is elastic, so wealthier consumers will pay." Evaluate this argument using the elasticity framework: (a) If luxury demand is elastic, what happens to revenue from a luxury tax? (b) Who actually bears the burden of a tax on a good with elastic demand and inelastic supply? (c) Does the elasticity analysis support or complicate the policymaker's equity argument? *(Tests: applying the revenue rule and incidence rule together; evaluating a policy claim that conflates legal incidence with economic incidence.)*

*10. The long run arrives.* The chapter's synthesis claims that "elasticity grows with time" — that short-run inelasticity is temporary. Identify one market where this claim is empirically questionable: a market where demand or supply has remained inelastic over a long period, despite the passage of time and the apparent opportunity for substitutes to emerge. Explain why the standard adjustment mechanism hasn't worked in that market. What feature of the market (search costs, regulation, network effects, physical constraints) is blocking the elasticity from growing? *(Tests: critical evaluation of the chapter's central empirical claim; applying the framework to its own limits.)*

## Chapter 07: Chapter 7 — Production, Costs, and Industry Structure
*Source: `chapters/07-production-costs-and-industry-structure.md`*

## Exercises

**Warm-up**

*1. Accounting vs. economic profit.* A restaurant owner takes home $80,000 a year after paying all explicit costs. She quit a management job that paid $95,000 to open the restaurant. She also invested $200,000 of her own savings, which would earn 5 percent ($10,000/year) if left in an index fund. (a) What is her accounting profit? (b) What are her total implicit costs? (c) What is her economic profit? (d) Should she stay in the restaurant business, based solely on this analysis? *(Tests: calculating economic profit; identifying and quantifying implicit costs.)*

*2. Diminishing marginal product.* A firm adds workers one at a time to a fixed factory. The first worker produces 50 units per day; the second produces 40 additional units; the third, 25; the fourth, 10; the fifth, 2. (a) Fill in a table showing number of workers, total output, and marginal product for each worker. (b) At which worker does diminishing marginal product first appear? (c) Is there a point where adding a worker reduces total output? What would that worker's marginal product be, and what does that tell you about the factory? *(Tests: computing marginal product from an output sequence; identifying diminishing returns; understanding zero vs. negative marginal product.)*

*3. Marginal pulls average.* Explain in your own words, in two sentences, why the marginal cost curve must cross the average total cost curve at its minimum — without using the word "mathematical" or "calculus." Use an analogy if it helps, but not the grade-test analogy from the chapter. *(Tests: conceptual understanding of the MC-ATC relationship; ability to explain it without formula.)*

**Application**

*4. Build the cost table.* A firm has fixed costs of $500/day. Variable costs at each output level are: 0 units = $0; 10 units = $100; 20 units = $180; 30 units = $240; 40 units = $320; 50 units = $430. (a) Compute TC, AFC, AVC, ATC, and MC at each output level. (b) At what output level is ATC minimized? (c) Verify that MC crosses ATC at approximately that point. Show all your work. *(Tests: constructing the full cost table from raw data; verifying the MC-ATC crossing rule numerically.)*

*5. Short run vs. long run.* A bakery currently has two ovens (capital, fixed in the short run). It is considering adding a third baker (labor). Separately, it is considering buying a third oven. (a) Which decision is a short-run adjustment and which is a long-run adjustment? Why? (b) If the bakery is already experiencing diminishing marginal product from its existing bakers, will adding a third baker increase average total cost, decrease it, or is the answer ambiguous? Explain using the cost curve logic. (c) What is the economic argument for buying the third oven instead? *(Tests: short-run vs. long-run distinction applied to a real business decision; connecting diminishing returns to the ATC curve.)*

*6. Fixed vs. variable cost matters.* Two competing food delivery apps have the same revenue per delivery: $8. App A has high fixed costs ($2M/month in software infrastructure) and low variable costs ($1/delivery in payment processing fees). App B has low fixed costs ($200K/month) and high variable costs ($5/delivery in contractor fees). Both currently do 500,000 deliveries per month. (a) Calculate each app's total cost and profit per month. (b) If monthly deliveries double to 1 million, recalculate. Which firm benefits more from growth? (c) Name the cost-structure feature that explains the difference. *(Tests: applying fixed vs. variable cost reasoning to a technology business; connecting cost structure to returns from scale.)*

**Synthesis**

*7. The bookstore autopsy.* The chapter argues that Barnes & Noble lost to Amazon because of a cost-structure mismatch, not predatory pricing. Use the specific language of this chapter — fixed costs, variable costs, marginal cost, economies of scale, LRAC — to construct the argument in full. What was the shape of each firm's LRAC? Why did Amazon's LRAC keep falling at outputs where Barnes & Noble's was flat or rising? Could Barnes & Noble have replicated Amazon's cost structure? What would it have taken? *(Tests: applying all chapter concepts to a single real case; reasoning about cost structure as a strategic constraint.)*

*8. Read an industry.* The pharmaceutical industry has very high fixed costs (R&D and clinical trials for a single drug can cost $1–2 billion) and very low marginal costs once a drug is approved (manufacturing a pill may cost pennies). (a) Sketch the shape of a drug company's ATC curve. (b) What does this cost structure imply about the industry's tendency toward concentration? (c) When a drug goes off-patent and generic manufacturers enter, what happens to industry ATC? Why can generics charge far less without losing money? Connect your answer to the fixed-cost structure of the original drug. *(Tests: applying cost-curve analysis to a high-stakes real industry; connecting fixed/variable cost split to pricing and entry dynamics.)*

**Challenge**

*9. Zero economic profit is not failure.* The chapter states that zero economic profit is the long-run equilibrium in a competitive market. A student reads this and says: "Then why would anyone start a business if the best you can hope for is zero profit?" Write a two-paragraph response that (a) explains what zero economic profit actually means in terms of the owner's income, and (b) explains what forces drive a *competitive* market toward zero economic profit in the long run, and why that convergence is not a tragedy but the expected behavior of a well-functioning market. *(Tests: distinguishing accounting from economic profit at the level of explanation, not just calculation; understanding long-run competitive equilibrium.)*

*10. When diseconomies don't arrive.* The chapter predicts that very large firms eventually face diseconomies of scale — coordination costs, bureaucracy, loss of visibility. Yet Amazon, as of 2024, employs over 1.5 million people and has continued growing with no obvious long-run average cost reversal. Propose two reasons why Amazon might have delayed or avoided the diseconomies-of-scale region that standard theory predicts. Then name one observable signal that would tell you diseconomies had finally set in, and describe how you would look for it in Amazon's financial data. *(Tests: critical evaluation of the LRAC model against a real counterexample; distinguishing "model is wrong" from "model's prediction is delayed.")*

## Chapter 09: Chapter 9 — Monopoly
*Source: `chapters/09-monopoly.md`*

## Exercises

**Warm-up**

*1. Name the barrier.* For each of the following, identify the primary barrier to entry — natural monopoly, legal monopoly, resource control, or network effects — and explain in one sentence why no other firm has entered: (a) The local natural gas distribution company; (b) Pfizer's patent on a blockbuster drug; (c) LinkedIn's professional network; (d) De Beers diamond supply in the twentieth century. *(Tests: classifying barrier types; connecting the mechanism to the real case.)*

*2. Build the MR table.* A monopolist faces the following demand: at $50, 1 unit; at $45, 2 units; at $40, 3 units; at $35, 4 units; at $30, 5 units. (a) Compute total revenue at each quantity. (b) Compute marginal revenue at each quantity. (c) Is there a quantity at which MR is negative? What does negative MR tell you about what happens to total revenue if the monopolist goes further? *(Tests: calculating MR; understanding the sign of MR as a signal about total revenue.)*

*3. Price taker vs. price maker.* In one paragraph, explain the difference between a price taker and a price maker. Use only the demand curve each firm faces to anchor the explanation — do not use the words "competitive" or "monopoly." *(Tests: understanding MR = P for price takers vs. MR < P for price makers through the shape of the demand curve, not through labels.)*

**Application**

*4. Find the profit-maximizing quantity.* A monopolist faces the demand and cost data below. Find the profit-maximizing quantity, the price charged, the economic profit, and the deadweight loss relative to the competitive outcome (where P = MC).

| Q | Price | TR | MR | TC | MC | ATC |
|---|-------|----|----|----|----|-----|
| 1 | $90 | | | $50 | | |
| 2 | $80 | | | $80 | | |
| 3 | $70 | | | $100 | | |
| 4 | $60 | | | $130 | | |
| 5 | $50 | | | $175 | | |
| 6 | $40 | | | $240 | | |

Fill in the table. Find where MR = MC. Identify the monopoly price and profit. Then identify the competitive quantity (where P = MC) and calculate the deadweight loss triangle. *(Tests: full MR = MC application; distinguishing profit rectangle from deadweight loss triangle.)*

*5. The patent trade-off in numbers.* A pharmaceutical company spends $800 million developing a drug. Without a patent, generic competition would drive the price to marginal cost ($2/pill) immediately after approval. With a 20-year patent, the company charges $100/pill and sells 2 million pills per year. (a) Estimate the company's annual economic profit during the patent period (use your judgment about implicit costs and capital). (b) How many years of patent protection does the company need before it recovers its R&D cost? (c) Does a 20-year patent seem too long, too short, or about right for this case? What would an economist argue that a longer patent costs society? *(Tests: connecting legal monopoly to the static-dynamic trade-off; applying DWL reasoning to a real policy case.)*

*6. Markup and elasticity.* Chapter 5 showed that a monopoly's markup depends on the elasticity of demand. A monopoly facing demand elasticity of 2.0 sets a markup of 100 percent over marginal cost. A monopoly facing elasticity of 1.5 sets a markup of 200 percent. (a) Explain in plain English why lower elasticity allows a higher markup. (b) A government is considering whether to regulate a pharmaceutical monopoly. The drug treats a rare condition with no substitutes (elasticity ≈ 0.3). What does the elasticity tell you about how large the markup likely is and what is happening to consumer surplus? (c) The same government is considering regulating a streaming service with elasticity ≈ 1.8. Is the welfare case for regulation stronger or weaker here? Why? *(Tests: connecting the elasticity chapter to monopoly pricing; applying the logic to two contrasting cases.)*

**Synthesis**

*7. Transfer vs. waste.* The chapter distinguishes between monopoly profit (a transfer) and deadweight loss (genuine waste). A student argues: "If the monopoly just raises price and consumers pay more, that's still a harm — why does it matter whether it's called a transfer or a loss?" Write a two-paragraph response that (a) explains the economic distinction between the two, and (b) explains why the distinction matters for policy — specifically, what a government can do about a transfer that it cannot do about the waste. *(Tests: the core welfare economics distinction; connecting it to policy instruments.)*

*8. The natural monopoly trilemma.* A city owns the only water utility. The city council is debating three pricing options: (a) let the utility set its own price to maximize profit; (b) require average-cost pricing; (c) require marginal-cost pricing with a government subsidy to cover losses. For each option, identify: the price relative to ATC and MC, whether the firm earns profit, whether allocative efficiency is achieved, and the political/incentive problems it creates. Which option minimizes total social cost? Justify your answer with specific reference to the cost curves. *(Tests: full application of the natural monopoly dilemma with all three regulatory options compared.)*

**Challenge**

*9. Creative destruction vs. antitrust.* Joseph Schumpeter argued that monopoly is temporary — that today's dominant firm is always vulnerable to tomorrow's disruptive entrant, and that breaking up monopolies reduces the profit that incentivizes innovation. The standard antitrust view says dominant firms use their position to block entry and should be constrained. Pick one specific current platform (Amazon, Google, Meta, Apple) and evaluate which view better fits the evidence. What would Schumpeter predict will happen to this firm's dominance over the next decade? What would a standard antitrust economist predict? What observable evidence would help you distinguish the two predictions? *(Tests: engaging with the static vs. dynamic trade-off in monopoly analysis; applying to a real case.)*

*10. When the model overpredicts.* The textbook model predicts that a monopoly maximizes profit by setting MR = MC and charging the demand-curve price at that quantity. But real monopolies often charge less than the simple model predicts — prices that leave consumer surplus on the table, below the monopoly-profit-maximizing level. Propose two explanations for why a real monopolist might price below the MR = MC prediction. Then consider: if monopolies routinely price below the theoretical optimum, does this make the case for antitrust intervention stronger or weaker? *(Tests: critical evaluation of the model against real firm behavior; connecting the discrepancy to the policy debate.)*

## Chapter 11: Chapter 11 — Monopoly and Antitrust Policy
*Source: `chapters/11-monopoly-and-antitrust-policy.md`*

## Exercises

**Warm-up**

*1. Compute the metrics.* An industry has firms with the following market shares: 35%, 28%, 12%, 8%, 7%, 5%, 3%, 2%. (a) Compute the CR4. (b) Compute the HHI. Show your squaring step. (c) By current DOJ guidelines, is this market unconcentrated, moderately concentrated, or highly concentrated? (d) A merger is proposed between the #1 firm (35%) and the #3 firm (12%). Compute the post-merger HHI and the HHI increase. Does this trigger detailed review? *(Tests: computing both metrics; applying DOJ thresholds; calculating a merger's effect on HHI.)*

*2. Per se or rule of reason?* For each of the following, identify whether it would be treated as per se illegal or evaluated under the rule of reason — and explain why the legal standard differs: (a) Two airlines agree to charge the same price on a specific route; (b) A shoe manufacturer requires retailers to sell only its brands; (c) A software company includes its own browser in the operating system; (d) A construction firm bids above cost on a government contract while colluding with a competitor on who will "win." *(Tests: distinguishing per se from rule-of-reason categories; knowing which standard applies to which practice.)*

*3. Averch-Johnson in plain English.* Explain the Averch-Johnson effect in two sentences without using the word "capital." Use only the logic of incentives and costs. Then explain: why doesn't the Averch-Johnson effect arise under price-cap regulation? *(Tests: conceptual understanding of the incentive distortion; connecting it to the difference between the two regulatory frameworks.)*

**Application**

*4. Evaluate a merger.* In 2011, AT&T proposed to acquire T-Mobile. At the time, the four major U.S. wireless carriers had approximate market shares of: Verizon 33%, AT&T 28%, Sprint 16%, T-Mobile 11%. (a) Compute the pre-merger HHI. (e) Calculate private saving (S_p = S − S_g) and verify the expanded identity S_p = I + (G − T) + NX. *(Tests: computing all components of the expanded identity from raw data; verifying the identity holds.)*

*2. Ricardian equivalence logic.* The government announces a $500 billion tax cut, to be repaid through higher taxes in 10 years. *3. Crowding out in two economies.* Economy A is a closed economy (no international capital flows). Economy B is open with unrestricted capital mobility. Both governments borrow an additional 3% of GDP. (a) In Economy A, what happens to domestic interest rates and private investment? (b) In Economy B, what happens to domestic interest rates, capital inflows, the exchange rate, and net exports? (c) In Economy B, is domestic investment crowded out? What is crowded out instead? (d) Which economy faces more crowding out of domestic investment from the same deficit? *(Tests: closed-economy vs. open-economy crowding-out mechanisms; the redistribution of crowding out to the trade balance.)*

**Application**

*4. The twin deficits in the 1980s.* Between 1980 and 1986, the U.S. federal deficit widened from roughly 3% to 5% of GDP. Over the same period, the U.S. current-account deficit widened from near balance to roughly 3% of GDP. (a) Using the identity S_p = I + (G − T) + NX, identify which terms changed. (b) Why did the trade deficit widen rather than domestic investment falling by the same amount? Trace the capital-flow and exchange-rate mechanism. (c) The Reagan administration was simultaneously cutting taxes and increasing defense spending. Which of these primarily drove the fiscal deficit? *5. Debt sustainability arithmetic.* The U.S. has $28 trillion in publicly held debt and a GDP of $28 trillion (debt-to-GDP ratio = 100%). The nominal interest rate on the debt is 4.5%. (b) Is the debt-to-GDP ratio rising or falling on this path, and by how much per year? (c) What primary surplus would be required to stabilize the debt-to-GDP ratio? (d) If nominal GDP growth rises to 5% (higher inflation or higher real growth), does the required adjustment get larger or smaller? *(Tests: the sustainability arithmetic; computing the required primary surplus; sensitivity to the growth rate.)*

*6. The 2009–2014 zero lower bound period.* The U.S. federal deficit averaged about 7% of GDP from 2009 to 2012. The Federal Reserve held the federal funds rate at near zero throughout this period. If so, through which channel? (c) If the Fed had not been at the zero lower bound (i.e., if rates were at 4%), how might the story have been different? (d) What does this episode imply about the reliability of the simple crowding-out model as a guide to policy? *(Tests: conditional crowding-out analysis; the role of monetary policy stance; connecting theory to evidence.)*

**Synthesis**

*7. Tariffs vs. fiscal consolidation for the trade deficit.* The chapter claims fiscal consolidation reduces the trade deficit more reliably than tariffs. (b) Trace the mechanism by which a tariff on $200 billion of Chinese imports affects the trade balance — including the exchange-rate offset. (c) In which case is the trade balance more likely to actually improve? (d) Under what conditions might tariffs work better than the standard analysis suggests? *(Tests: comparing two policy instruments using the savings-investment identity; the exchange-rate offset applied to both.)*

*8. Intergenerational equity.* The chapter notes that current borrowing leaves bills for future taxpayers. (a) In what sense is deficit spending an intergenerational transfer? (b) Does it matter whether the deficit funds current consumption (transfer payments, war costs) vs. productive investment (infrastructure, education)? How does the type of spending affect the intergenerational equity argument? (c) The U.S. spends substantially more on Medicare and Social Security (benefits concentrated in older generations) than on education and infrastructure (benefits concentrated in younger generations). Does this pattern make the intergenerational transfer argument stronger or weaker? *(Tests: intergenerational incidence of government borrowing; connecting the type of spending to the equity argument.)*

**Challenge**

*9. Japan's debt paradox.* Japan has maintained government debt-to-GDP above 200% for over a decade without a debt crisis, despite having nominal interest rates near zero and modest growth. (a) Apply the r < g vs. r > g framework to Japan's situation — which condition holds and how does it keep Japan's debt stable? (b) Japan borrows in yen, which it can print. Why does this matter for sustainability? (c) Japan's domestic savings rate is high and Japanese investors hold most Japanese government debt. How does this differ from Greece's situation, and why does it matter? (d) Is Japan's experience evidence that high debt is always sustainable, or does it depend on conditions that may not be replicable elsewhere? *(Tests: applying the sustainability framework to Japan as a real counterexample to standard predictions; identifying the conditions that make Japan different.)*

*10. The confidence problem.* The chapter notes that debt sustainability is partly a function of fundamentals and partly a function of market confidence. Greece's crisis occurred at debt-to-GDP around 100% while Japan has managed 200%+ without crisis. (a) Name three specific differences between Greece and Japan that explain why confidence held for Japan but not Greece. (b) The chapter notes that U.S. reserve-currency status creates strong demand for U.S. Treasuries — but reserve-currency status is itself based on confidence. Is this circular? ## Chapter 33: Chapter 33 — International Trade

**Warm-up**

*1. Identify comparative advantage.* Country A can produce 10 tons of steel or 5 tons of cloth per worker per year. Country B can produce 4 tons of steel or 8 tons of cloth per worker per year. (a) Which country has absolute advantage in steel? In cloth? (b) Compute the opportunity cost of steel (in cloth) for each country. (c) Compute the opportunity cost of cloth (in steel) for each country. (d) Which country has comparative advantage in steel? In cloth? (e) At what range of price ratios (steel per cloth) would both countries gain from trade? *(Tests: the core comparative advantage calculation; identifying the mutually beneficial price range.)*

*2. Why absolute advantage is irrelevant.* Country X is the most productive in the world — its workers produce 50% more of every good than any other country's workers. (a) Does Country X have comparative advantage in every good? Explain. (b) Can Country X benefit from trading with a less productive country? Use the brain-surgeon analogy to explain. (c) A politician argues: "Country X shouldn't import anything because its workers are better at making everything." What is the error in this reasoning? *(Tests: the irrelevance-of-absolute-advantage result; identifying the common political error.)*

*3. Gains from trade arithmetic.* Use the U.S.-Brazil wheat-sugar example. Each country has 100 workers. (a) Without trade, with a 50/50 labor split, compute each country's output and the world total. (b) With full specialization, compute each country's output and the world total. (c) If trade occurs at 1 wheat = 0.8 sugar, and the U.S. trades 150 tons of wheat, compute each country's final consumption. (d) Verify both countries gain compared to the no-trade scenario. (e) Is 0.8 sugar/wheat within the mutually beneficial price range? *(Tests: computing before/after trade outcomes; verifying that a specific price ratio is in the mutually beneficial range.)*

**Application**

*4. Intra-industry trade.* The U.S. and Germany both have large automobile industries. In 2023, the U.S. exported roughly $13 billion of motor vehicles to Germany and imported roughly $35 billion of German vehicles. (a) The Ricardian model doesn't predict this pattern — explain why. (b) Which of the three intra-industry trade mechanisms (product differentiation, economies of scale, supply chains) best explains U.S.-Germany auto trade? Provide a specific example for each. (c) If the U.S. imposed a 25% tariff on German vehicles, would the Ricardian model predict this harms the U.S.? What does the intra-industry trade model predict? *(Tests: explaining why standard theory fails for intra-industry trade; applying the three mechanisms; connecting to tariff analysis.)*

*5. iPhone supply chain value-added.* Apple's iPhone is manufactured across multiple countries. Suppose a $1,000 iPhone has the following rough value-added distribution: design/IP (U.S.): $400; chip fabrication (Taiwan): $150; display (South Korea): $80; assembly and logistics (China): $80; components from other countries: $150; Apple retail margin: $140. (a) If this phone is counted as a Chinese export to the U.S. of $1,000, how much of the $1,000 actually represents Chinese value-added? (b) How does this affect the interpretation of the U.S.-China trade deficit? (c) A tariff on iPhones assembled in China would primarily hurt which parties — Chinese workers, Chinese value-added, American consumers, or Apple shareholders? Use the value-added breakdown. *(Tests: supply-chain fragmentation and the gap between gross trade flows and value-added; tariff incidence in supply chains.)*

*6. Comparative advantage shifts.* In 1960, South Korea had comparative advantage in labor-intensive manufacturing (textiles, light assembly). By 2000, it had comparative advantage in capital-intensive industries (shipbuilding, steel, semiconductor chips) and consumer electronics. (a) What changed that shifted South Korea's comparative advantage? Name at least two factors. (b) Does this shift challenge or support the Ricardian framework? Explain. (c) A country with comparative advantage in low-wage assembly today might lose that comparative advantage as wages rise. What does this imply for countries currently specializing in low-wage manufacturing? *(Tests: dynamic comparative advantage; the connection between development and evolving trade patterns.)*

**Synthesis**

*7. Static vs. dynamic case for free trade.* A small country is considering eliminating all tariffs on manufactured goods. (a) Make the static comparative-advantage case for free trade — what gains are available from specialization? (b) Make the dynamic case for free trade — what gains beyond static reallocation might materialize over 20 years? (c) Make the strongest case *against* free trade for this country. Under what conditions does the case for protection have merit? (d) Overall, is the static or the dynamic case for trade more compelling for a small developing country? *(Tests: synthesizing both arguments; naming the legitimate exceptions to the free-trade case.)*

*8. The Heckscher-Ohlin extension.* The Heckscher-Ohlin model says a country exports goods that use its abundant factor intensively. The U.S. is capital-abundant and labor-scarce relative to many trading partners. (a) What does Heckscher-Ohlin predict about U.S. export patterns? (b) The "Leontief Paradox" found that the U.S. exported labor-intensive goods in the 1950s despite being capital-abundant. What does this suggest about the Heckscher-Ohlin model's predictions? (c) How does the rise of human-capital (skilled workers, engineering) as a production factor help reconcile the paradox — is skilled labor more like physical capital or unskilled labor in Heckscher-Ohlin terms? *(Tests: applying the factor-endowment model; engaging with a famous empirical anomaly; connecting to modern trade patterns.)*

**Challenge**

*9. Infant industry argument.* The chapter mentions "infant industries" as one condition where the gains-from-trade story weakens. South Korea's industrial policy in the 1960s–1980s explicitly protected infant industries (steel, shipbuilding, semiconductors) from foreign competition while they developed. Those industries are now globally competitive. (a) What is the theoretical argument for infant industry protection — why is it an exception to free-trade advice? (b) The problem with infant industry policy is the "sunset problem" — what is it? (c) South Korea's infant industry policy appears successful in retrospect. Does this validate the policy choice, or could the same development have occurred without protection? What evidence would distinguish between these? (d) Is there a more efficient policy than import protection for achieving the same goal of building up an infant industry? *(Tests: the infant industry exception; the sunset problem; evaluating a historical case; alternative policy instruments.)*

*10. The distributional problem.* The chapter notes gains from trade are real but "not evenly distributed." A steel town loses 2,000 jobs when a U.S. tariff on imported steel is removed. Meanwhile, 300 million American consumers each save $30 per year on cheaper steel-containing goods. (a) Compute the aggregate consumer gain and compare it to the aggregate worker loss (assume average steel worker earned $70,000/year). Who gains more in aggregate? (b) Why does the concentrated loss politically dominate the diffuse gain, even though the aggregate gain is larger? (c) A Trade Adjustment Assistance program provides two years of income support and retraining to displaced workers. At what annual cost would such a program fully compensate the steel workers? Is this cost below the consumer gain? (d) If the answer to (c) is "yes, consumers gain more than it would cost to compensate workers," why doesn't this compensation happen routinely? What political economy problem blocks it? *(Tests: distributional arithmetic of trade; the political economy of concentrated vs. diffuse interests; the logic and limits of compensation.)*
