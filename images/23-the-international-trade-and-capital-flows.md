# Image briefs — Chapter 23: International Trade and Capital Flows

## Hero image
**Concept.** A cargo ship at a port at dusk, stacked containers in shadow. Waterline visible. Mood: the physical face of a financial flow most observers don't see directly. No people.

## Figure 1 — U.S. current account 1980–2024
**Concept.** Time series. Y-axis: current account balance as % of GDP. X-axis: 1980–2024. Visible: small surplus 1980, deficit emerging 1980s, larger deficit through 2000s peaking around −6%, narrowing somewhat 2010–2020, then varying.
**Where:** in §"What gets counted in the trade accounts."

## Figure 2 — Savings-investment identity diagram
**Concept.** A schematic showing two flows. Domestic savings flows into the financial system. Domestic investment draws from the financial system. The gap (when investment > savings) is filled by foreign capital. The capital inflow shows up as the trade deficit. Annotation: "S − I = NX. Always."
**Where:** in §"The savings-investment identity."

## Figure 3 — Trade balance vs. level of trade — country comparison
**Concept.** Scatter plot. X-axis: exports + imports as % of GDP (level). Y-axis: exports − imports as % of GDP (balance). Different countries plotted: Singapore (high level, surplus), U.S. (low level, deficit), Germany (medium-high level, surplus), Japan (medium level, balanced), Brazil (low level, mixed).
**Where:** in §"Trade balance vs. level of trade."

## Figure 4 — Capital outflow shock dynamics
**Concept.** Sequence diagram. (1) Foreign investors lose confidence, (2) capital flows out, (3) currency depreciates, (4) interest rates rise, (5) imports become more expensive, (6) exports become cheaper, (7) trade balance shifts toward surplus, (8) GDP and employment may fall in short run. Each arrow labeled.
**Where:** in §"When trade deficits become problems."
