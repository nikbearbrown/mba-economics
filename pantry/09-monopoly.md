# Pantry — Chapter 9: Monopoly

## Cold-open scenes
- East India Company / Boston Tea Party 1773. 342 chests, 92,000 lbs of tea.
- Water utility, AT&T (historical), De Beers — natural-monopoly and resource-control examples.

## Worked examples
- Demand schedule with falling price: $10/1, $9/2, $8/3, $7/4. TR and MR derivation. MR < P proven.
- Natural-monopoly regulation dilemma — MC pricing leads to losses; AC pricing breaks even.

## Concept specifications
- **Monopoly = one seller, no close substitutes.**
- **Monopoly persistence requires barriers to entry.** Without a barrier, profit attracts entry.
- **Five barrier types:** natural (cost-curve), legal (patent/license), resource control, network effects, predatory pricing.
- **MR < P for monopoly** — the price-cut-on-existing-units takes a bite.
- **Monopoly produces less, charges more** than competitive industry would.
- **Deadweight loss ≠ profit transfer.** Profit transfer is distributional; deadweight loss is destroyed value.

## Trade-offs named
- Patent protection: incentive to innovate vs. monopoly cost during patent period.
- Natural monopoly: economies of scale vs. consumer welfare.
- Antitrust intervention: cost of forbearance vs. cost of breakup (regulatory error in either direction).

## Counter-claims taken positions on
- "Monopoly is just market success." → Sometimes — but the consumer cost is real.
- "Free markets prevent monopolies." → Only when entry is genuinely free; barriers can hold for decades.
- "Patents are pure rent extraction." → No. The dynamic R&D incentive is real.
