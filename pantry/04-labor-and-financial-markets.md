# Pantry — Chapter 4: Labor and Financial Markets

## Cold-open scenes
- 2020 nurse demand surge — boomers + ACA expansion → demand right, wages up, travel-nurse market explodes.
- 2021–2023 mortgage-rate climb — 3% to 7%, used as four-step worked example.

## Worked examples
- Minneapolis-St. Paul RN labor market (2020 BLS, ~41,000 RNs).
- Federal deficit → demand for capital shifts right → crowding-out reasoning.
- ATM example — technology can shift labor demand left in the long run, but did not for bank tellers in the short run.

## Concept specifications
- **Labor market = market for *hours of a particular kind of work in a particular place*.** Not a single market.
- **Wage** is the price; **interest rate** is the price in the financial market.
- **Marginal revenue product** of labor — flagged for Ch 14.
- **Monopsony** — one or few large employers can suppress wages below competitive level.

## Named tools
- Labor demand and supply curves.
- Financial-capital demand and supply curves.
- Minimum wage (price floor).
- Usury cap (price ceiling).
- Crowding out (government borrowing competes with private borrowers).

## Trade-offs named
- Minimum wage: redistribution to employed workers vs. employment effects on marginal workers; depends on competitive assumption.
- Usury cap: protection of borrowers from predatory rates vs. rationing of credit to riskiest borrowers (who may turn to gray market).
- Government borrowing: short-run macro stimulus vs. long-run crowding out.

## Counter-claims taken positions on
- "Minimum wage always cuts employment" — true direction in competitive market, weakly observed in many real markets.
- "Usury caps protect the vulnerable" — protect *some*; ration *others* out of formal credit.
- "Technology takes jobs" — sometimes; sometimes the opposite for decades; depends on demand for the firm's output.
