# Pantry — Chapter 16: Information, Risk, and Insurance

## Cold-open scenes
- Akerlof 1970 lemons paper, rejected three times before publication, eventual Nobel.
- ACA 2010 individual mandate as adverse-selection response.

## Worked examples
- Used-car lemons unraveling.
- Insurance contract menu (high deductible vs low deductible) as screening device.
- Driver who insures and then drives more aggressively (moral hazard).

## Concept specifications
- **Asymmetric information ≠ imperfect information.** Asymmetric: one side knows more.
- **Adverse selection** = sorting at contracting stage; private info determines who accepts the contract.
- **Moral hazard** = behavior change *after* contracting; informed party adjusts behavior.
- **Insurance pools risk** by using statistical predictability across many independent loss events.
- **Insurance market vulnerable to both** adverse selection (high-risk over-represented) and moral hazard (insured behave differently).

## Named tools
- Lemons model (Akerlof).
- Signaling (Spence: education as signal).
- Screening (insurance menus).
- Mandatory pooling (social insurance).
- Deductibles, copays, monitoring as moral-hazard responses.
- Capital requirements (banks).

## Trade-offs named
- Coverage generosity vs. moral hazard.
- Mandate-coercion vs. selection-collapse.
- Government provision (politically durable, mixed insurance/redistribution) vs. private (cleaner pricing, vulnerable to selection).
- Privacy vs. risk-sharing (more information reduces selection but invades privacy).

## Counter-claims taken positions on
- "Markets always sort efficiently." → No, when information is asymmetric they can collapse.
- "Moral hazard means insurance is always bad." → No, insurance produces enormous welfare gains; moral hazard is a manageable trade-off.
- "Government insurance is just transfers." → No, often it's solving a pure market-failure problem that private insurance can't.
