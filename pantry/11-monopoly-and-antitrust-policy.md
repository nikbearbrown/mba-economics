# Pantry — Chapter 11: Monopoly and Antitrust Policy

## Cold-open scenes
- Standard Oil 1911 breakup (34 firms; 90% refining share before).
- AT&T 1982 breakup; price of LD calls fell 90%+ in following 15 years.
- Google antitrust prosecution (ongoing 2024).

## Worked examples
- HHI calculation with hypothetical market shares.
- Cost-plus utility regulation with gold-plating incentive.
- Microsoft 1990s case as canonical "behavioral remedy that achieved less than hoped."

## Concept specifications
- **Sherman Act (1890), Clayton Act (1914), FTC Act (1914)** — three pillars of U.S. antitrust.
- **HHI = sum of squared market shares.** DOJ thresholds: <1500 unconcentrated; 1500–2500 moderate; >2500 highly concentrated.
- **Per se illegal** vs. **rule of reason** — bright line for some violations (price-fixing) vs. balancing test for others (tying, exclusive dealing).
- **Cost-plus regulation** — incentive to inflate costs; **price-cap regulation** — incentive to cut quality / underinvest.
- **Regulatory capture** — Stigler 1971; regulators identify with regulated industry.

## Named tools
- HHI and CR4 concentration measures.
- Merger review process.
- Behavioral vs. structural remedies.
- Cost-plus vs. price-cap regulation.
- Regulatory capture (Stigler).

## Trade-offs named
- Enforcement costs (mistakes in either direction) vs. benefits.
- Structural breakup (durable, blunt) vs. behavioral remedy (flexible, evadable).
- Cost-plus (efficiency-killing) vs. price-cap (regulator-information-dependent).
- Regulation (capture risk) vs. unregulated monopoly (extraction).

## Counter-claims taken positions on
- "Antitrust always works" → No. Microsoft case is a counter-example.
- "Antitrust never works" → No. AT&T breakup was a structural success.
- "Regulators serve consumers" → Sometimes; capture is a real and documented risk.
